THE PILLAR
Volume I  ·  The Hidden Strategist
Episode  8
Heartstring

A name is not what you are called.
 
A name is who stood beside you
when it was given.
 
Three men.
Three fathers.
Three names.
 
None of them born with the one they carry.

────────────────────────────

### **Part I. The Morning After**

Taeyang cut Eunsik's hair before the forge fire was lit.

It was the first practical problem of the day and he solved it the way he solved everything — without discussion. A prince's hair was bound a certain way. A commoner's was not. A man who had walked out of a palace cell the previous afternoon could not sit in a forge yard with the topknot of the inner court on his head while the district went about its morning.

Eunsik sat on the stool. Taeyang stood behind him with the shears.

"Hold still."

"I am holding still."

"You are holding still like a prince. Hold still like a man who does not care what his hair looks like."

Eunsik considered this. Then his shoulders dropped a fraction.

"Better," Taeyang said, and cut.

Jung Hae watched from the doorway. He said nothing. He had learned long ago that the fastest way to make two people into something was to put them in the same small task and leave.

He went to light the fire.
 
By the time the forge was warm, Eunsik looked like a young man from the eastern quarter who had come to learn the trade from his older brothers. Plain clothes. Short hair. Soot on his hands, because Taeyang had handed him a bucket of ash and said carry this, and he had carried it.

He stood in the middle of the forge and looked at his hands.

"I have never been dirty," he said. Not complaining. Noting.

"You have now," Taeyang said. "Stand further from the door. People who are new stand in the middle. People who belong stand where the work is."

Eunsik moved.

Jung Hae, at the anvil, did not look up. But the corner of his mouth moved.

### **Part II. What He Was Told**

They worked through the morning because the district expected the forge to work.

Three men at a forge where there had been two. The district noticed, the way districts notice everything, and filed it under the only explanation that cost nothing: a cousin from the provinces. Come to learn. Half the forges in the eastern quarter had one.

At midday they ate in the yard.

Taeyang ate the way he always ate. Eunsik ate slowly, the way a person eats who has spent months being fed through a slot and has not yet remembered that food can be a thing you choose.

Taeyang watched him. Then he said —

"He told me about you once."

Eunsik looked up.

"Four years ago. In this yard. We were sitting where you are sitting." Taeyang tilted his head toward Jung Hae, who was inside, banking the fire. "He said there was someone he wanted me to meet. Another brother. He said you did not know you were our brother yet."

Eunsik was very still.

"What else did he say?"

"That you were the best person he had ever met. In the palace or outside it." Taeyang went back to his rice. "He does not repeat things. So I remembered it."

Eunsik looked at the forge door.

He thought about a storage corridor. A cat. A young man who had handed him the western province ledgers and never once told him he was clever, because telling him would have been a waste of a sentence that could carry something useful instead.

He thought: four years ago I was in the rear quarters writing to an empty room. And he was sitting here telling a stranger I was the best person he had ever met.

He did not say any of this.

He said, "He has never said that to me."

"He will not," Taeyang said. "That is not how he says things."

"How does he say them?"

Taeyang thought about it.

"He hands you a ledger," he said.

### **Part III. The Name**

It came that night, at the low table, with the lamp between them.

Eunsik had been quiet since the meal. Not the quiet of a man with nothing to say. The quiet of a man arranging a question so that it would come out clean.

He asked it.

"What do I call you?"

Jung Hae looked at him.

"The record says Nam Jung Hae. The general gave you Su. The district calls you Byeon Mae." Eunsik's voice was level. "I have called you nothing for five years because I thought you were dead. I would like to know what to call you now."

The lamp burned.

Taeyang did not move. He knew this was not his question. He also knew he wanted to hear the answer.

Jung Hae was silent for a long moment.

Then he said —

"My name is not Nam Jung Hae."

He said it plainly. The way he said everything that was true.

"Nam was my father's name. He died of fever when I was ten. My mother followed him. I carried the name for two years on a grain sack in the lower market because it was the only thing they left me."

He looked at Eunsik.

"When I was twelve, your father sat across from me in a small room in the east wing and said: from today, your name is Gu Jung Hae. There was no document. No court. The chief eunuch was the only witness. Your father said: to this palace you are an attendant, but in here you are my son, and my sons carry my name."

A pause.

"I am Gu Jung Hae. I have never said it aloud to anyone but him."

The forge was completely quiet.

Eunsik looked at the man across from him.

He thought about his father. The plain dark robe. The particular way he had of giving things without ceremony, so that you did not understand what you had been given until years later.

He thought: he gave me a brother and did not tell me. He gave this man my name and did not tell him he was giving me a brother.

He thought: of course he did. That is how he gave things.

He said —

"Then you are Gu."

"Yes."

"By my father's word."

"Yes."

Eunsik nodded once. Slowly. The nod of a man setting something down in the right place.

"Gu Jung Hae," he said. Testing the weight of it. The way Jung Hae had tested it once, at twelve, across a different table.

"That is my brother's name."
 
Taeyang spoke then. Quietly.

"He is Su by my father's word."

They both looked at him.

"Su Minho served under the general fourteen years. He said: if something happens, the general will come. The general came. He gave me a brother and gave the brother our name." Taeyang shrugged, the gesture of a man stating facts. "So he is Gu by your father. Su by mine. And Byeon Mae to the district, because the district needs something to call him."

He looked at Jung Hae.

"Three names. Three fathers. All of them dead." A pause. "I do not think that is an accident. I think that is what it costs to be you."

Jung Hae looked at the lamp.

He did not answer. There was no answer that would not be smaller than the thing Taeyang had said.
 
After a while Eunsik said —

"And me? What do I carry?"

"Your father's name," Jung Hae said. "The only one of us who has it by blood."

"That is not what I asked."

Jung Hae looked at him.

"You carry the one thing your father wanted carried," he said. "You carry what he thought a king should be. He told me that when you were seven. He said: that one will be great. Not because he is clever. Because he sits across from people."

Eunsik's face did something.

He looked down at his hands — the clean hands with soot on them.

"I did not know he said that."

"He said it once," Jung Hae said. "He did not repeat things either."

### **Part IV. The King's Fear**

Gu Seongmin had the painted screen removed on the third day of his reign.

The screen had stood behind the throne of Joseon for one hundred and forty years. Eight panels. Cranes and pines. Every king since the dynasty's founding had sat with his back to it.

Seongmin could not.

He had been in the throne room when his brother fell. He had been standing in the second row, in the position reserved for the elevated prince, and he had seen Taeju's hand go to his throat and had seen nothing behind him — nothing, with his own eyes, in his own court — and some part of him that was older than reason had never recovered from the nothing.

So the screen came down.

The lamp-tenders' gallery behind it, a narrow space that no one on the court had thought about in a generation, was bricked shut by masons who worked through the night and were paid to forget what they had done.

The rear wall was solid stone now.

Seongmin sat with it at his back and still turned his head.

He turned it during reports. During audiences. During the oath of a provincial governor who knelt and spoke for four minutes while the King of Joseon looked, twice, over his own shoulder at a wall.

The court saw this.

The court said nothing. The court had learned, under the previous king, what saying something cost.
 
He stopped holding full sessions in the second week.

Ministers were received in the eastern study instead — three at a time, with guards at the door and the King seated in a corner, where there was no behind. Reports were delivered in writing when they could be. When they could not, they were delivered short.

The kingdom's business slowed to the pace of one frightened man's willingness to be in a room.

In the provinces, the forty-seven empty positions became fifty-two.

On the northern border, General Ahn's reinforcement request went unanswered for twenty days because the King would not sit through the meeting at which it was to be discussed.

The shadow had killed one king.

It was ruling the next one without touching him.

### **Part V. The Queen's Room**

The Queen Dowager received the investigation's final report in her own quarters, alone.

She read it once. She did not need to read it twice. Forty days. Forty-three witnesses. A wound from behind. Nothing.

She set the report down.

Her name was Yun. She had entered the palace at seventeen as a consort of the second rank, and she had spent thirty-one years learning that a palace is not a place. It is a ledger. Everything in it is entered somewhere. The only question is whether you know where to look.

She had known where to look for thirty-one years.

Now she looked.
 
She did not begin with the throne room. The throne room had been searched by men who were looking for a person, and there had been no person to find. She began with the question the investigators had not been trained to ask.

Who gained?

Not Seongmin. He was hers, and he was so afraid of a wall that he could not govern. Not the exiled princes in the south — she had men in their household; they had learned of Taeju's death from a trader four days after it happened. Not the border kingdoms; they had not moved.

She drew a line under the list.

Then she wrote one more name beneath it, because she was thorough, and because a name that cannot gain can still be a door.

*The ninth prince.*

He had been in a cell. He had walked out of it during the eleven minutes when every guard in the detention level was running toward a throne room. Through a door that had been unlocked, the report said, not forced. With a key of the administrative pattern.

He could not have killed a king from a cell.

But someone had opened the cell, and someone had known to the minute when to open it, and both of those someones had stood very close to the thing she was looking for.
 
She sent for the registries.

Not the current ones. The old reign's. The twenty-second year. She had Taeju's search records brought with them — the months he had spent, in the first year of his reign, looking for a strategist who did not exist. She had thought him foolish then. She read his notes now with a different expression.

He had found an anomaly and stopped at it.

*Nam Jung Hae. Junior attendant, inner east wing storage. Deceased. Fever. Autumn, Year 22.*

A servant with too many document transfers. A fever record that was perfect. A body released to no family.

Taeju had sent men into the districts and found nothing and let it go.

The Queen Dowager did not let things go.

She turned the page, and the page beneath it, until she reached the household records of the rear eastern quarters — the reports of court ladies and guards from the first week of the new reign, filed by people positioned to report what they saw.

*The ninth prince wept on reading the servant registry. The name Nam Jung Hae. Lady Yeon held his hand.*

She read the line three times.

A seven-year-old prince who had followed a cat into the east wing storage corridor and had spent five years going back.

A servant who died of fever the week the King died.

A prince who cried for a servant — exactly as much as a prince should, and not one tear more.

A king who stood up in his own throne room, with nothing behind him, and died.
 
She sat back.

On the table beside her was a Go board. She had played for thirty-one years. Her late husband had never once asked her to.

She picked up a black stone and placed it on the board, at the edge, in the corner no one guards.

"A dead servant," she said, to no one. "Who taught a prince. Who walked out of a cell."

She looked at the stone.

"Find me the lamp-tenders' roster," she said. "The twenty-second year. And the name of every man who held a key to the detention level this spring."

The court lady at the door bowed and went.

The Queen Dowager looked at the board for a long time.

Then she placed a second stone beside the first.

She had been playing against a dead king for thirty-one years.

She had just understood that the dead king had not been playing alone.

### **Part VI. Heartstring**

The forge, late. The fire banked. Three men and a lamp.

Eunsik told them about the ghost.

He had not meant to. It came out of a question Taeyang asked about the eastern study — what the King was like, in the sessions — and Eunsik answered it the way he answered everything now, completely, and the completeness carried him somewhere he had not intended to go.

"He saw my father," he said. "In the corner of the room. Standing there in a plain robe. Laughing."

Jung Hae went still.

"Not every night. The deep hours. He threw things at the corner. He told no one. I knew because the physician's records changed shape, and because he went to the northern wing on the nights the laugh was worst." Eunsik's voice did not alter. "He was not hurting my mother. He was hurting the thing in the corner. She was just the nearest thing my father had loved."

The lamp burned.

Taeyang said nothing. This was not his to speak into.

Jung Hae said, very quietly —

"He laughed."

"Quietly. The way you laugh at something that has gone exactly as you predicted."

Jung Hae looked at the fire.

He thought about a market corner. A man in a grey robe who had been read for twenty-two mornings and had laughed, once, at the end of it — the quiet exhale of a man who had met something he had not prepared for.

He thought: the only time I ever heard him laugh was when I beat him.

He thought: and his son heard it every night for three years and could not make it stop.

He thought: he was never a cruel man. But he was a man who had seen the whole board. And a man who has seen the whole board, standing in a corner watching his son lose it piece by piece — I do not know what else he would have done.

He did not say any of this.

He said, "I am sorry you were in the room for that."

Eunsik looked at him. "I was not in the room. I was three corridors away, answering questions so that he would stay out of hers."

"I know."

"It did not work."

"I know."

A silence.

Then Eunsik said the thing that had been underneath everything since the empty room.

"I have her last letter. The real one. Not the ones the physician wrote. I did not know it was the last one when I read it." He looked at his hands. "It says: I am well. I am glad about the plum tree."

He stopped.

"That is all she had room to say. Because they read everything. So she said the plum tree. And I wrote back about the plum tree for three years."

No one spoke.

Taeyang reached out, without looking, and set his hand flat on the table between them. Not on Eunsik's hand. Beside it. Close enough to touch. The way a stone is placed next to two others.

Eunsik looked at the hand.

He did not take it.

He put his own hand down beside it.
 
After a while Jung Hae said —

"Your father gave me two words. Fill it. The general gave me one sentence. Everything I have built is in you. She gave you a plum tree."

He looked at Eunsik.

"That is three instructions. None of them are small. We are going to carry all three."

"How?"

Jung Hae reached for the board. He lifted the cover. Two stones at the center.

"The Queen Dowager reads records," he said. "She is reading them now. She will find a servant who died of fever and a prince who cried for him. She will find a key and a roster. She is better than her son was. She will pull the thread."

"Then we cut it."

"No." Jung Hae placed a third stone, close enough to touch the two. "A thread is only dangerous if the other end is where she thinks it is. She reads records. So we give her a record."

Taeyang looked at the board. Then at his brother.

"What record?"

Jung Hae looked at the three stones.

"The one the chief eunuch did not have time to write," he said. "Nam Jung Hae has been dead for five years. It is time he was buried properly."
