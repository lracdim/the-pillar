A Dead Man's Grave

The best lie

A record is only paper.
A grave is a record
with a body in it.

### **Part I. Seven Seals**

The letter arrived at the eastern study in the fourth week of Seongmin's reign, carried by an envoy who did not kneel.

He was from Buk-Yeon, the largest of the northern kingdoms — a man of fifty with a soldier's hands and a diplomat's patience, who had crossed the border with an escort of forty and had been allowed to, because the garrison commander who should have stopped him had no orders and no stomach for deciding.

The letter carried seven seals.

Seven kingdoms. Three to the north, two to the west, two across the southern strait. Kingdoms that for five years had tested Joseon's borders one skirmish at a time and had, each time, found the garrison thinner, the supply slower, the commander newer than the time before.

They had compared notes.

The letter was short. It said that the seven kingdoms, mindful of the long friendship between their houses and the house of Gu, and concerned by the disorder within Joseon's provinces, proposed a treaty of protection. Joseon would pay, each year, an amount of grain and silver that the letter specified to the last measure. In return, the seven kingdoms would guarantee Joseon's borders.

Against whom, the letter did not say.

It did not need to.

The envoy added one thing aloud, in the flat voice of a man delivering a figure rather than a threat.

"My king asks for an answer by the first snow. After the first snow, the passes are open until spring."

He bowed — the shallow bow of one court to another, not of a subject — and withdrew.
 
Seongmin read the letter in the corner of the study with his back to two walls.

He read it twice. He did not understand it the second time any better than the first, because the thing he did not understand was not in the letter. It was in the room — the way the three ministers present were not looking at him. They were looking at the door.

Waiting for the person who would decide.

She came within the hour.

### **Part II. The Price of a Year**

The Queen Dowager read the letter standing.

"Tribute," she said.

"Protection," the treasury minister said carefully.

"Tribute." She set the letter down. "How much of it can we pay?"

The minister looked at the figures. He had been one of the three who read the truth in the throne room on the day Taeju died, and he had been expecting to be dismissed for it every day since. He was not. The Queen Dowager had kept him, because a man who had told the truth once in front of forty-three witnesses was a man whose numbers could be used.

"The grain, barely. The silver, not without the eastern reserve."

"And the eastern reserve is —"

"Forty thousand seok on paper. Less in fact."

She looked at him.

"How much less."

"I do not know, Your Highness. The last man who tried to find out was reassigned."

She nodded, slowly. She knew who had reassigned him.

"Pay it," she said.
 
Seongmin stood up.

"We cannot —"

"We can pay or we can be invaded by seven armies in the spring with a garrison that lost to one of them twice last month." She did not raise her voice. She had never needed to. "Paying buys a year. In a year we will have found what we need."

"What do we need?"

She looked at her son — the elevated prince, the puppet who could not sit with his back to a wall — and for a moment something in her face was almost kind.

"An army that wins," she said. "Your father had one. It did not come from his generals."

She turned to the ministers.

"Draft the acceptance. The King will seal it tonight. And bring me the detention-level key roster. I asked for it nine days ago."

"Your Highness — the clerk who held the administrative key records resigned the week before the session. The records are incomplete."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Resigned," she said.

"Family obligations in the provinces, Your Highness. The usual language."

"The usual language." She picked up the envoy's letter again, and did not look at it. "Find me the clerk."

They did not find the clerk.

Cho Baekhyun had been moved three days before the session to a county two hundred li south, under a name the network had held ready for him since the day he took the position. His desk had been clean. His replacement was a competent man with no past and no questions.

The Queen Dowager read the report of the empty desk and understood, for the first time, the shape of what she was reading.

Not a man. Not a conspiracy of ministers. Something that pulled its own pieces off the board the week before you reached them.

She wrote one line in her own hand and underlined it.

*It removes what I am about to find.*

Then, beneath it —

*So it knows what I am about to look for.*

### **Part III. What the Landlord Said**

The Queen's men reached the lower district on the eleventh day.

They were not the men Taeju had sent, five years earlier, to walk through markets asking for a young man with no family. These were better. Quiet men in merchants' clothes who did not ask questions. They listened for the shape of an answer in what people said about other things.

They found the landlord on the third day.

He was older now. He still asked no questions, and was still happy to answer them when the silver was right.

Yes. Five years ago. A young man. Thin. Straight-backed. A Go board under his arm, always. He paid a month ahead and never spoke. The autumn the old King died.

"And then?"

The landlord shrugged.

"The cough took him that winter. Many that winter. He had no people. I sold the board for the burial fee and the parish put him in the eastern field with the others." He scratched his jaw. "He had a name on the rent ledger, if you want it. I keep the ledgers. Paper is cheap and the magistrate asks."

He brought the ledger.

*Nam Jung Hae. One room. Paid.*

And beneath it, in a different ink, in the same hand —

*Deceased. Twelfth month. Burial fee paid from effects.*
 
The eastern field was where the capital buried the people it did not count.

Rows of low mounds. Wooden markers where someone had paid for a marker, bare earth where no one had. The Queen's men walked the rows of the twenty-second year's winter with the parish record and found the mound — a marker, weathered grey, the characters cut shallow and softened by five winters of rain.

*Nam.*

Nothing else. A surname. A pauper's mark.

They reported this.

The Queen Dowager read the report, and then she did something her son would not have done and her late husband would have.

She went herself.

### **Part IV. The Grave**

She stood in the eastern field at the hour before dusk in a plain cloak with two guards, and she looked at the mound for a long time.

Then she had it opened.

The guards did not like it. She did not ask them to like it. They dug.

The body was five winters in the ground. A young man's frame — the long bones of someone who had been seventeen or eighteen. No effects. A burial cloth of the cheapest weave. Nothing on him and nothing with him.

A dead man.

She looked at the bones in the failing light.
 
She thought: the chief eunuch was thorough. This is more thorough.

She thought: a fever record in the palace, a cough in a boarding house, a mound in the paupers' field. Three records. Three places. Five years apart in the finding and perfectly aligned in the telling.

She thought: if he were dead, the records would disagree somewhere. Records of real deaths always disagree somewhere. People are careless about the dead.

No one had been careless about this one.

She looked at the marker — grey, soft-edged, five winters old, and exactly as old as it should be. She had expected to find it too new. She did not. Whoever had done this had not done it last week.

Whoever had done this had done it five years ago, the week the boy went into the ground on paper, so that the grave would be waiting if anyone ever came.

The Queen Dowager stood over the open grave of a stranger and understood that she was looking at a move made before she had known there was a game.
 
"Close it," she said.

The guards closed it.

She stood a moment longer.

"You buried him well," she said. To the field. To the man she could not see.

Then she turned and walked back toward the eastern gate, and the two guards, who had not understood a word of it, followed.

### **Part V. What the Grave Was For**

Kwon Taesik had dug the grave himself, in the twelfth month of the twenty-second year.

He had not known what it was for. The instruction had come from the general himself, in the first winter after the humiliation, through the channel the general had built for exactly this — and the general had said, in the margin, that the instruction was not his. It said: a body from the winter dead, unclaimed, of the right age. A marker. A surname only. A landlord who keeps ledgers, paid to remember a cough. Record everything. Then forget it.

He had done it in two days and never thought about it again.

Five years later, a merchant he did not know stopped in the eastern field and stood over the mound for the time it takes to say a prayer, and Kwon Taesik, who was selling lamp oil forty paces away, saw it and understood what the grave had been for the entire time.

He did not go to the forge. He sent the signal.
 
"She went herself," Taeyang said, reading it.

Jung Hae was at the anvil. He did not stop working.

"Of course she did."

"She opened it."

"She would have to. A record she did not open would be a record she had not read."

Taeyang set the signal down. "And now?"

"Now she knows two things." Jung Hae turned the blade in the tongs and looked at the edge. "That the servant is dead. And that whoever buried him is not."

Eunsik, at the bellows, said quietly, "You gave her the thread and the end of the thread at once."

"I gave her a true thing." Jung Hae set the blade down. "A boy named Nam Jung Hae died the winter the old King died. She can dig in that field until spring. She will not find him anywhere else, because he is not anywhere else."

He looked at his brothers.

"The name she needs is not the one in the ground. She does not know the one she needs. And no record in the palace has ever held it, because your father never wrote it down."

Eunsik understood then.

Gu Jung Hae. A name given in a small room with one witness, and the witness had died holding a corridor.

The Queen Dowager could read every ledger in Joseon.

The name was not in any of them.

### **Part VI. The Board Is Not the Palace**

The tribute decree went out under the King's seal on the twenty-sixth day.

Grain carts left the eastern reserve for the northern pass under escort. Silver followed in sealed chests. The criers announced a treaty of friendship with the seven kingdoms, and the people of the capital, who could read a grain cart as well as any minister, understood what friendship cost and said nothing.

Jung Hae read the decree on the public board at the edge of the market, the way he had read another announcement there, five years before.

He read it twice.

Then he went back to the forge and said —

"We leave in three days."

Taeyang looked up. Eunsik looked up.

"She bought a year," Jung Hae said. "She thinks the year is hers — to find the hand behind the victories and put it to work for her son. She will spend it reading the capital, because the capital is the only board she has ever played on."

He lifted the cover from the Go board and set it on the table between them.

"Your father told me the board is the palace. He was right, for his reign. He was the king, and the king was on the board." He placed a stone. Not at the center. At the far edge — the northern edge, where the lines ran out. "The palace is one square now. She holds it. Let her."

"Then what is the board?" Eunsik asked.

"The kingdom." Jung Hae placed a second stone beside the first. "Seven armies will come through the northern pass in the spring whether the silver is paid or not, because the silver tells them the throne is weak and weak thrones are invaded. The treaty did not buy Joseon a year. It bought us one."

Taeyang leaned forward. "Us for what?"

"Thirty-one men are not an army. They are the officers of one." Jung Hae looked at him. "The soldiers who walked out behind the general did not stop being soldiers. They are in the provinces, in the counties the shadow cleaned, and they remember who they were. They need a place to stand and a name to stand under."

He looked at Eunsik.

"Not mine. I am a dead servant and a blacksmith. They will not follow a shadow into the pass." A pause. "They will follow the son of the king they won under."

The forge was quiet.

Eunsik looked at the two stones at the northern edge.

He thought about the rear quarters, and the cell, and three years of letters to an empty room, and a cat on a cold corridor floor.

He thought: he is handing me a ledger.

He said, "Then we go north."
 
"And the Queen?" Taeyang asked.

"She has one year to find me." Jung Hae covered the board. "I have one year to make the army her son will need. If she finds me first, she gets a strategist. If I finish first, she gets a kingdom she no longer rules."

He stood.

"Either way, the board gets filled."

### **Part VII. One White Stone**

The forge was empty on the fourth day.

The Queen's men came on the fifth, following the last thread she had — not a name, but a trade. The court physician's report on the throne-room wound had said one thing the investigators had passed over: the blade was not of palace make. Its edge had been drawn by a smith who knew what an edge was for. The Queen Dowager had read that line once, and it had meant nothing, and then she had read it again after the grave and it had meant: forges.

There were sixty forges in the capital and its districts.

Her men had reached eleven when they came to the one in the eastern quarter whose fire was cold.

The forge master, who owned the building and had rented it for the better part of six years — first to a quiet older man and his two sons, then, after the square, to the sons — said the young men had gone back to the provinces. Family. The usual language. Good workers. Quiet. The older one had a Go board.

The Queen's men searched the forge.

Tools in their places. The sleeping mats gone. A low table. The third forge stone, which rocked slightly when you stood on its left edge, and beneath it a hollow — dry, swept, holding nothing.

And on the anvil, placed at its center as if someone had set it down and simply not picked it up, one white Go stone.
 
They brought it to her.

The Queen Dowager held it in her palm in the room where she had played for thirty-one years against a man who never answered.

A white stone. Smooth. Old. The kind that had been handled for a very long time by someone who turned it in his fingers while he thought.

She looked at it for a long while.

Then she set it on her own board — not among her black stones in the corner, but at the center, alone, where she had never placed anything, because the center was where her husband had played.

"Nam Jung Hae is dead," she said quietly. "You went to a great deal of trouble to tell me so."

She looked at the stone.

"So. What did you name yourself?"
 
On the northern road, four days out, three men walked with a cart of forge tools and a worn Go board wrapped in cloth against the cold.

The pass was two weeks ahead. Beyond it, the garrisons that had once not lost a battle for five years. Beyond those, seven kingdoms counting silver and sharpening for spring.

Eunsik walked beside Jung Hae. Not behind him. Beside.

"She will find the name eventually," he said.

"Yes."

"What will you do when she does?"

Jung Hae looked at the road ahead — the long grey line of it, running north into the places where the board had no edge.

"Answer to it," he said.

*He had been Nam, on a grain sack, for two years.*
*He had been nobody, in a corridor, for five.*
*He had been Byeon Mae, at a forge, for five more.*
 
*A king had given him a name in a small room*
*and died in that room*
*so that the name would outlive him.*
 
*No ledger in Joseon held it.*
 
*He was carrying it north*
*to the one place it would finally be spoken aloud —*
 
*in front of an army.*
